The problem, from one patient's side

Meet Amparo. Nothing hurts. Nothing looks wrong.

Amparo is 69, with type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure. Her blood test says her kidneys filter well, so her results are called "acceptable".

But a urine test found albumin, a protein that should not be there. That is often the first sign that diabetes and high blood pressure are damaging the kidneys. A second high result came in a letter from her private insurer and was typed into a note, where no filter looks.

Her record has no kidney diagnosis. She is not on the blood-pressure medicine that also protects the kidneys. The warning sign is hiding behind a normal blood test.

Amparo is made up: she is patient 251 in our synthetic demo. The numbers below are real.

That is about 10.6 million US adults, an undercount, since a third of people had no lab result. The same gap exists in Spain: 54 % of kidney disease in 5,009 adults with type 2 diabetes in Spanish primary care was undiagnosed, "mainly in patients with isolated eGFR or uACR abnormalities" (DIAMOND2, 2026).

Why it matters

There is a clear next step. It just doesn't happen.

  • Albumin in the urine predicts a higher risk of death, independently of the kidney blood test (105,872 people, 14 population studies; Matsushita et al., Lancet 2010).
  • The international kidney guideline: "We recommend that treatment with an ACEi or an ARB be initiated in patients with diabetes, hypertension, and albuminuria" (KDIGO 2022, Rec. 1.2.1).
  • But one urine test is not a diagnosis: about half of high results are high again on a second sample 10 days later. So the right first step is simple: repeat the urine test.

Why it gets missed

Attention, not knowledge

The blood test is the number most people read as "the kidney result", and it looks fine. The urine result sits elsewhere in the record, sometimes only in a note or a letter, and goes out of date quietly.

No one has time to check every chart, every morning.

What we built

Three steps. The doctor makes every decision.

  1. It reads the list

    Every morning, it checks everyone with diabetes or high blood pressure with rules from kidney guidelines (KDIGO, Valencian primary-care protocol), signed by the project's physician. A small open AI model also reads the notes for results typed there; the code checks every sentence it points to.

  2. It prepares the next step

    For each person it finds: the test order, one line for the notes and a date to review the result, with the reason, the number and the date.

  3. The doctor signs

    One click to sign, or "no" with a reason. If a test is already ordered or was repeated recently, the person is not listed again.

Who benefits, and who could be harmed

Honest about what it can and can't do

Who benefits

  • People like Amparo: a kidney problem found while it can still be slowed.
  • Family doctors: the checking is done before the clinic opens.

Who could be harmed

  • Extra tests: about half of repeat orders come back normal.
  • False reassurance: not on the list is not "cleared"; it only sees the record.
  • Wrong setting: the numbers are from the US.

Synthetic patients only; no real patient data anywhere. NHANES is public CDC/NCHS data. Built with AI assistance (Claude for code and analysis; a small open model, Qwen3-4B, reads synthetic notes and published case reports); every clinical rule and threshold was signed by the project's physician.
